# ADR 0016: NVENC directly, and epochs that continue instead of starting over

**Status:** accepted (2026-11-18), for M12d: part 1 built (0.41.0); part 2 waits on the spike below. It amends [ADR 0003](0003-epoch-streams-for-video.md): an epoch may begin with a frame that continues from one the viewer already decoded, not only with a keyframe.

The author's choices (2026-11-18):
- **measure first:** what a keyframe costs the picture, before building anything (below);
- **reference frame invalidation is in M12d's scope,** with the wire revision it needs;
- **Windows hosts taking GPU frames only:** H.264 and HEVC from the capture's textures, and the host's own stream window. FFmpeg keeps frames in memory, AMD, Intel, software and every Linux host;
- **two pull requests:** M12d-1, the encoder and bitrate changes in place, merged first; then the spike, then M12d-2, the continuation epochs;
- **the spike's bar is any clear gain** over a keyframe, with no decoder error (below);
- **a stream window that fell behind decoding asks to continue,** not for a keyframe.

Settled while building M12d-1 (0.41.0, [results](../m12d-results.md)):
- **The header is nv-codec-headers' `sdk/13.0` at commit `ced4f8eb`,** the one BtbN's build of our FFmpeg 8.1 compiles against (`scripts.d/50-ffnvcodec.sh`), vendored in `third_party/nv-codec-headers/`; the bindings are generated by `cargo xtask gen-nvenc-bindings` with `_WIN32` undefined (the header then declares its own `GUID`, no `windows.h`), and bind no functions: the two entry points are loaded from the DLL. The struct versions and GUIDs, which bindgen can't take, are written by hand and checked against the header by tests.
- **"The same stream" needed FFmpeg's own defaults,** not only our options: `refs` 0 (the driver picks the reference count: 3 here, not 1), `h264_nvenc`'s `s12m_tc` on (`enableTimeCode`, which sets the HRD's `time_offset_length`), the slice mode repeated in each picture, and the VUI's `bitstreamRestrictionFlag` for H.264 only. With them, the parameter sets match FFmpeg's byte for byte and the decoded pictures exactly (PSNR ∞), in both codecs (`tests/gpu.rs`).
- **No device lock:** FFmpeg's D3D11 NVENC takes none around its calls (only a CUDA context is pushed, which D3D11 doesn't use), so ours doesn't either.
- **A failure falls back per viewer:** `pipeline::platform`'s `DirectNvenc` opens FFmpeg's NVENC on the same GPU frames when ours fails to encode, from a keyframe, so GPU frames stay on for the other viewers; when ours can't open, FFmpeg's opens as before.

## Context

Two things cost us a keyframe today that NVENC itself doesn't need:

- **Every bitrate change.** FFmpeg 8.1's NVENC wrapper (our pin, n8.1.3) sets `resetEncoder` and `forceIDR` on any change of the average bitrate, the maximum or the VBV (`libavcodec/nvenc.c`, `reconfig_encoder`). So the rate controller batches increases into steps of at least 15%, at most one a second (`COSTLY_MIN_STEP`), and every decrease costs one too.
- **Every recovery.** A catch-up (`RateAction::CatchUp`), a resume after an outage (`Resume`), and a viewer that fell behind decoding or failed to decode (`KeyframeRequest`) all start a new epoch with a keyframe. FFmpeg calls NVENC's `nvEncInvalidateRefFrames` nowhere, and has no long-term reference frames.

**What a keyframe costs** (`couch-cli capture-test --keyframe-every 1` and `--bitrate-every 1 --bitrate-to 12`, NVENC fed from memory, 2026-11-18):
- Its size doesn't change: the one-frame VBV holds a forced keyframe to about one frame's budget (1.0×).
- **Its picture does.** In Witcher 3 at 20 Mbit/s, a forced keyframe was 5.2 dB of luma PSNR worse than the 10 frames before it in H.264 (SSIM −0.098), 4.4 dB in HEVC (−0.076). It healed over 7 to 10 frames: within 0.5 dB from the 8th in H.264, and still 0.66 dB short at the 9th in HEVC. On synthetic pictures the dip was 4 to 8 dB. Points halfway between the keyframes moved by less than 0.6 dB.
- **A bitrate step's reset** gave the same dip (Witcher 3: −4.7 dB in both codecs) and a keyframe of 1.04× the budget in H.264 and 1.43× in HEVC. On mandelbrot it was 2.5× to 3.6×: the reset forgets the VBV's state.

While the bitrate climbs, that is a soft picture about once a second.

**What doesn't apply to us.** Moonlight and Sunshine use reference frame invalidation because their video crosses lossy UDP: a lost packet corrupts a frame, and later frames must stop referring to it. Our epochs travel on reliable QUIC streams, so a lost packet only delays (ADR 0003). We discard frames only on purpose, when the host resets an epoch's stream, and then it knows exactly which frames the viewer has: the viewer reports each epoch's bytes received (`ReceiverReport::epoch_bytes_received`), and the host wrote every frame's bytes.

**What NVENC offers** (Video Codec SDK, `nvEncodeAPI.h`):
- `nvEncReconfigureEncoder` with `resetEncoder` and `forceIDR` both 0 changes the bitrate in place, where `NV_ENC_CAPS_SUPPORT_DYN_BITRATE_CHANGE` says so.
- `nvEncInvalidateRefFrames(timestamp)` marks that frame, and every frame reconstructed from it, unusable as a reference; the next frame predicts from the newest valid frame left, or is coded intra if none is (`NV_ENC_CAPS_SUPPORT_REF_PIC_INVALIDATION`).
- **Long-term reference frames** (`enableLTR`, per-picture mode: `ltrMarkFrame`, `ltrMarkFrameIdx`) stay in the decoded picture buffer until replaced, beyond the short-term list's reach (`maxNumRefFrames`, 16 at most for H.264). The encoder's output says whether a frame was marked (`ltrFrame`, `ltrFrameIdx`) and which long-term frames it used (`ltrFrameBitmap`). The header warns that faster presets may keep fewer long-term frames than asked; we use p1.

The reach matters: a catch-up fires only once the viewer is more than max(150 ms, 2 × minimum round trip) behind, beyond a round trip and the report's age. The newest frame it received is then usually more than 16 frames old at 60 fps, always at 120. An outage is 500 ms of silence. Without long-term frames, invalidation would almost always leave nothing to predict from.

**Every decoder we have parses through FFmpeg.** Software, D3D11VA, D3D12VA and VAAPI decoding all use FFmpeg's H.264 and HEVC parsers, which manage the reference lists; the hardware decodes only slices. How a decoder copes with frames that never arrived (gaps in H.264's `frame_num`, references missing from an HEVC picture's reference set) is therefore one behaviour to prove, not four.

## Decision

### 1. NVENC without FFmpeg (M12d-1, no wire change)

A new crate, **`couch-codec-nvenc`**, the only one that calls NVENC:
- it loads `nvEncodeAPI64.dll` at run time (`NvEncodeAPIGetMaxSupportedVersion`, `NvEncodeAPICreateInstance`); its bindings come from nv-codec-headers (MIT), generated once and committed like FFmpeg's, at the API version FFmpeg's pinned build uses, so no driver that runs FFmpeg's NVENC today loses it, and it gets a section in the third-party notices;
- **a session on the capture's D3D11 device,** its pool's BGRA textures registered (`NV_ENC_INPUT_RESOURCE_TYPE_DIRECTX`, `NV_ENC_BUFFER_FORMAT_ARGB`) and mapped per frame. The rules of "Frames on the GPU" carry over unchanged: an encoder reopens when `GpuFrame::source` changes, keeps the pool's textures alive until it closes, never sees a texture freed or swapped in place;
- **the same stream as today:** p1, ultra-low-latency tuning, CBR with a one-frame VBV, an infinite GOP, no B-frames, no lookahead, SPS and PPS with every IDR, H.264 High and HEVC Main, and the VUI FFmpeg writes for RGB input (BT.601 matrix, limited range). Viewers see no difference but the bitrate's;
- **bitrate changes in place,** and `EncoderCaps::bitrate_change_costs_keyframe` false, so the rate controller applies increases above 3% at once, as it already does for such an encoder;
- **tried first** wherever `FfmpegEncoder::open_gpu` is called today (`pipeline::platform`, the host's own stream window, `couch-cli probe`); if it fails to open or to encode, `FfmpegEncoder` takes over as now, for that viewer alone, and GPU frames stay on. `COUCH_HOST_NVENC_DIRECT=0` keeps FFmpeg's, to measure against;
- **the device lock:** whether NVENC's calls need the device's multithread lock (the capture, the HDR conversion and the other encoders share the device) is checked against the SDK's guidance and FFmpeg's use first, then measured.

### 2. Continuation epochs (M12d-2, a wire revision)

**An epoch begins either with a keyframe, or with a frame that continues from one the viewer already decoded,** named in the epoch's preamble. Whenever the host would start an epoch with a keyframe to recover (a catch-up, a resume, a viewer's request), it may continue instead.

**The host's side** (a sans-IO module in `couch-media`, beside the rate controller):
- it keeps, per viewer, the last few seconds of frames sent: epoch, sequence number, the byte where each ends in its epoch's stream, the encoder's timestamp, and whether NVENC's output marked it long-term. From each report's `epoch_bytes_received` it knows the newest frame the viewer received whole;
- **long-term marking, two slots:** one holds the newest marked frame the viewer is known to have received; the next mark goes into the other slot, and only once the previous mark was received, at most every 100 ms. The frame the viewer is known to hold is never overwritten, so a recovery always has something to predict from, however far behind the viewer is;
- **to continue from frame F** (the newest frame the viewer received whole): reset the old epoch's stream (`SKIP_AHEAD`) as now, invalidate every frame encoded after F that NVENC may still refer to, and open a new epoch whose preamble names F. NVENC then predicts from F if it is still in its short-term list, else from the long-term frame the viewer holds, else codes an intra frame. Whichever it does, a viewer that decoded F cleanly holds the reference;
- **it falls back to a keyframe** when the viewer said it can't continue in this codec, the encoder can't (FFmpeg's, or a driver without the capabilities), it has no frame the viewer received in this stream, F is from before the encoder's last reopen, or the viewer asks for a keyframe because it holds nothing.

**The viewer's side** (the stream window, which alone knows what its decoder holds):
- it remembers which frames it decoded since its decoder last broke: an error, a reopen (another codec, D3D12VA given up, a restart of the window), or a frame dropped undecoded because a keyframe was awaited. Frames merely skipped from showing were still decoded and count;
- **it accepts a continuation from F only if it decoded F in the decoder's present state;** otherwise it drops the epoch's frames and asks for a keyframe, saying it can't continue from F;
- **when it falls behind decoding** (a frame waited more than 200 ms), it stops decoding and asks the host to continue from the last frame it decoded, rather than for a keyframe. After a decode error it asks for a keyframe, as now.

**The wire** (`PROTOCOL_MINOR` 22, a minor version):
- `EpochPreamble` gains `continues: Option<{epoch u32, seq u32}>`: the frame the epoch continues from;
- `KeyframeRequest`'s reasons gain `FellBehind { holds: {epoch, seq} }` (continue from there if you can) and `CantContinue` (a continuation named a frame I don't hold);
- `SessionJoin` gains `continues: u8`, codec bits like `codecs`: the codecs in which this viewer accepts continuations. A viewer whose decoder turns out not to cope in a codec leaves its bit unset, and that viewer gets keyframes;
- the viewer IPC's video frame header gains the frame an epoch's first frame continues from.

Nothing else changes: a new viewer, a new stream, another codec or size, a decoder error and the host's own stream window still start from a keyframe; the receiver report is as it is.

### 3. A spike decides whether part 2 is built

Part 1 makes the encoder; before any wire change, a test on it (`couch-cli`, NVENC directly, frames dropped on purpose, then continued) must show:
- **NVENC does it at p1:** its output marks long-term frames and uses them after an invalidation (`ltrFrame`, `ltrFrameBitmap`), in H.264 and HEVC;
- **FFmpeg's decoders cope,** software, D3D11VA and D3D12VA here and VAAPI and software on Linux (a Linux viewer watches Windows hosts): no decode error, and each decoded picture after the continuation as close to its source as the frames before;
- **it is worth it:** in Witcher 3, with a few hundred milliseconds of frames dropped, the picture after a continuation is clearly better than after a keyframe (whose dip was about 5 dB), well beyond what points with nothing happening move.

If NVENC won't keep long-term frames at p1, or a decoder can't follow, part 2 stops there (or narrows to the codecs and decoders that work, through `SessionJoin::continues`).

## Consequences

- **Good:** bitrate changes cost nothing to the picture, so the rate controller can follow the path smoothly instead of in 15% steps; recoveries keep the picture instead of a soft keyframe, and need no keyframe after an outage. G4's criterion becomes "one recovery" rather than "one keyframe".
- **Two encoders for NVIDIA:** ours on GPU frames, FFmpeg's as its fallback and for frames in memory. Both must keep the stream identical for viewers.
- **More unsafe code** around NVENC's API: the `windows-ffi-pitfalls` skill applies, and the encoder's tests run only with an NVIDIA GPU (`gpu-tests`), as `tests/gpu_input.rs` does now.
- **The host keeps per-viewer state about frames** (a few seconds' worth, bounded), and trusts a viewer's word only about that viewer's own stream: a frame it names is used only if the host sent it and NVENC can still refer to it.
- **An epoch no longer always starts with a keyframe.** `docs/protocol.md`'s epochs, the viewer's assumption that a new epoch's first frame is a keyframe, and the gates change with it.
- **AMD and Intel get none of this** until their encoders' own APIs are tried on their hardware (M12a).
